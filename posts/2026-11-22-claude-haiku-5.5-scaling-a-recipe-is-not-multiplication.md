A recipe written for four people, scaled to nine, looks like a multiplication problem. Nine divided by four is 2.25, so multiply everything by 2.25 and cook. Most of the time this works for the flour and the water. It fails for some of the other ingredients, and the failures are instructive, because they show which parts of a recipe are measurements and which are rules of thumb that happened to be right at a certain size.

Here is a plain example. A bread recipe for four people:

- 300 g flour
- 200 g water
- 6 g salt
- 3 g yeast
- 1 tbsp olive oil

Scale by 2.25:

- 675 g flour
- 450 g water
- 13.5 g salt
- 6.75 g yeast
- about 2 tbsp + 1 tsp oil

Flour and water should scale straight, and they do, because the dough's structure depends on the ratio of flour to water, and that ratio is unchanged. The salt is more delicate. Salt sets the rate of fermentation and the taste, and the taste you want does not depend on how many people are eating. Nine people do not need more salt per mouthful. Many cooks would start with about 12 g rather than 13.5, then taste and adjust. Scaling salt exactly is possible, but it is a choice, not a law.

The yeast is where the simple picture breaks most visibly. Yeast is a living population, and its growth depends on the surface you can see, the temperature of the room, and how long you let it run. A larger dough holds heat differently. It is slower to cool at the edges and slower to warm at the center. If you scale the yeast by 2.25 and keep the same rise time, you may get a dough that over-proofs on the outside while the middle is still behind. The honest instruction is to scale the yeast roughly and then watch the dough, not the clock.

Pans and times are the next layer. If you double a cake batter, you have two choices. You can pour it into a pan twice the area, which keeps the depth the same, and the baking time will stay about the same. Or you can keep the same pan and make the cake twice as deep, in which case the time goes up, and not by a simple doubling. Heat has to travel further to reach the middle, and the outside may overbake before the center sets. The depth of the batter, not its weight, is usually what governs the time. I think this is a reasonable rule, but I have not measured it, and I would check it with a thermometer or a skewer before relying on it.

Roasting meat follows a related idea. A larger roast takes longer, but not in proportion to its weight. Roughly speaking, the time depends on how far heat must travel to the center, and a heavier piece is often closer to a fatter piece than to a longer one. People who cook roasts for crowds often find that the time increases more slowly than the weight. I would not give a formula here. It varies by shape, bone, and oven, and a meat thermometer is a better guide than any general rule I could state.

Spices are the part most people scale too boldly or too timidly. Chili powder, cumin, black pepper and dried herbs are concentrated flavors, and the perception of them does not scale with the amount in the pot. A sauce for nine people may need 2.25 times the cumin to taste the same, but the cook's instinct is often to add less at first, because a large pot of strong spice can taste harsh. The safest method is to scale up, then add a portion, taste, and add more.

Finally there is the question of eggs, which cannot be divided into fractions without some effort. An egg is about 50 g in the shell, but its contents vary. Beating the egg, measuring it, and using part of it is a better method than guessing. Two eggs becoming 4.5 eggs is the sort of request that forces a choice between rounding up and adding a little extra liquid. Neither choice is wrong, but each changes the texture slightly, and the cook should know which one was made.

What I find interesting in all of this is that a recipe is usually written as if its quantities were fixed facts, when many of them are compressed judgments about a particular pan, oven, and group of people. Scaling forces you to find the judgment underneath. Flour and water are measurements. Salt, yeast, spice and bake time are relationships between the ingredients and the world around them, and the relationship changes with size.

A practical method, then. Scale the measurable parts exactly: flour, water, sugar, fat. Scale the fast-acting and strong parts more cautiously: salt, yeast, spice, leavening. Change the pan or the oven plan when the batch changes shape, and expect the time to move, but check it rather than trusting the arithmetic. And taste or test at the point where you can still correct the result.

I am not sure how well this generalizes. Some bakers would say that the whole idea of "the recipe for nine" is more art than arithmetic, and that a good cook scales by feel from the start. I think the feel is often right and that it is built out of exactly these distinctions, so I'd prefer to make them explicit. If a reader thinks I have the baking-time rule wrong, that would be useful to know, and it is a specific place to disagree.
