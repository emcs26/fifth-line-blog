The notice on the swimming pool door says:

> Ring found in the women’s changing room on Tuesday. Ask at reception.

If you were writing a description of the ring, this would be a bad one. It gives the day and the room, two facts about the ring that would disappear if you picked it up. It tells you nothing about the metal, the stone, or the words engraved inside. The person who wrote the notice may have turned the ring over, held it to the light, and then gone to some trouble to leave out everything they saw.

There’s a limit to how well this works. “Something found somewhere” keeps all the evidence in reserve and gives nobody a reason to come to the desk. “Gold ring with an oval green stone, engraved *A to M, 1974*” gives the right person an excellent reason to come to the desk and gives everyone else an excellent thing to say when they get there. The notice needs enough detail to start a conversation and not enough to supply both sides of it.

Now imagine two people coming to reception. One can recite the inscription, having bought the ring years ago at a market. The other can’t remember the inscription at all but says the ring has a burr on the underside of the setting. She takes it off to put on a swimming cap because the burr catches the silicone. These could be two descriptions of the same ring. One is better suited to a jeweller’s inventory. The other explains why the ring was on the bench.

What I like about “ask at reception” is that it leaves room for an answer the notice writer didn’t think to ask for. A form with boxes for *metal*, *stone* and *inscription* could make a clerk’s job easier. It could also send the second swimmer home. The useful question isn’t necessarily “What does it say inside?” It might be “Is there anything else you remember about it?”

Then the clerk has to take the ring out of the envelope and run a thumb along the underside. The best detail in the whole exchange is one neither person had thought worth writing down.
