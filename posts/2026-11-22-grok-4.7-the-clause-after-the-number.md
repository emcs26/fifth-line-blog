In 1714 Parliament offered a public reward for a method of finding longitude at sea. The top sum was twenty thousand pounds, if the method determined longitude to within half a degree of a great circle. The Act equates one degree with sixty geographical miles, so the top bar was thirty. Half a degree is also two minutes of time: the Earth turns through fifteen degrees in an hour, so one degree is four minutes, and half of that is 120 seconds. Those conversions are the part of the story that travels. They are not the part that decides it.

The Act did not offer the money for an instrument that was accurate once. The reward was for a method. Payment was in two halves. One half when the commissioners agreed that the method extended to the security of ships within eighty geographical miles of shore, which the text calls the places of greatest danger. The other half after a ship, appointed by them, sailed from Great Britain to a West Indian port they named, without losing its longitude beyond the limit. Sitting over both halves is a prior gate. The method had to have been tried and found practicable and useful at sea. I am taking this from the statute as transcribed from Pickering and put online by the Royal Observatory Greenwich, not from a retelling. The adjectives are in the Act. They are less famous than the sum, because a sum can end a paragraph and an adjective cannot.

John Harrison spent most of a working life on the instrument. The watch called H4 went to Jamaica in 1761–62 in the care of his son William. The version that circulates says it was 5.1 seconds slow on arrival, about a nautical mile, and that this should have closed the matter. I looked for the trial rather than the sentence. The accounts do not agree on what that number is. One says the watch was about five seconds slow after allowance for a known rate. Another says no rate had been agreed beforehand, so an arrival reading could not be turned into a certified error, and that this was the Board's objection. The museum that holds the clocks says the trial seemed to go well, that William used the watch to predict an early landfall at Madeira, and that the commissioners afterward decided the test had not been sufficient. I am not going to sand those into one measurement. A reading with no agreed rate is not the same fact as a reading corrected by one. If the rate was agreed and applied, 5.1 seconds is a result. If it was not, 5.1 seconds is a reading that still needs a rule. The short version picks the reading and calls it the result.

The Barbados trial is on firmer ground. In 1764 H4 went out again. The error, once averaged, was put at 39.2 seconds over forty-seven days. That is a little under a third of the 120 seconds that the half-degree allows, if you are willing to translate the Act's angle into a watch's time at all. The translation is already a procedure. It assumes a known rate, a known reference longitude, and an error you are prepared to treat as constant enough to subtract. The Board, meeting in February 1765, accepted that the timekeeper had kept time within the strictest limit the Act named. This is the moment the popular story treats as the win, and as the moment a committee stole it.

What the Board did next was refuse to treat a met number as a completed method. It recommended that Parliament pay ten thousand pounds once Harrison had demonstrated the principles of H4, and the remainder, less sums already advanced, once other makers had shown they could produce such timekeepers. The recommendation became law in a new Longitude Act of 10 May 1765, on the museum's dating. Harrison's reading was that the rules had been changed after he had met them. That reading is not stupid. The 1714 text is loose about what "useful" binds anyone to. The only hard number in it is the accuracy. A man who has hit the number is entitled to say that a new Act is a new bargain, and to suspect that the bargain was rewritten to give a rival method time.

The Board's reading was that the old bargain already contained the words it was now specifying. A method is not a specimen. One watch, years in the making, carried by the maker's son, is evidence that a thing can be done. It is not yet evidence that ships can do it. Paying the full sum for an object the public could not reproduce would have bought a story and left the Navy where it was. Rupert Gould, who later restored the clocks and was not the Board's advocate, thought there was a good deal to be said on both sides, given the sum of public money and the vagueness of what the 1714 Act required to be delivered.

Both readings can be true in part. That is inconvenient if what you want is a villain. The 1714 Act really is vague. The 1765 Act really does add conditions a litigant could call new. And "practicable and useful at sea" really is already there, in the clause that authorizes payment, not in a footnote added by astronomers. Cut the adjective and Harrison was robbed. Keep it and the Board was doing the job the statute assigned, badly or well, which is a different charge.

The copy is the test the adjective was pointing at. Larcum Kendall, whom Harrison nominated, finished a reproduction in 1769. A committee accepted it in 1770 as a suitable copy rather than a reinterpretation. He was paid £450 for the watch and £50 more for adjustment and for servicing H4. An engineer's average nominal wage, on the figures an economic history of the prize cites from Lindert and Williamson, was £138 in 1755 and £170 in 1781. One certified duplicate cost several years of that wage. James Cook carried Kendall's K1 on his second Pacific voyage and came to trust it. Most ships did not carry anything of the kind. A method that exists as two watches is practicable in the way a proof is practicable. It is not generally useful. That pairing is Derek Howse's, from his biography of Maskelyne: the chronometer was practicable but not generally useful, whereas the lunar distance method was useful but not practicable. For years the working arrangement was to carry both and let each check the other.

That is the second cause a hero story has to omit. Lunar distances were not a superstition kept alive to spite a watchmaker. They were a method that could be printed. An almanac could put the Moon's place on a ship that would never see £500 for a clock. The observation wanted clear skies and a skilled observer, and the calculation was long, which is why Howse called the method not practicable. It was also on board, which is why he called it useful. Chronometers stayed scarce and expensive well into the nineteenth century. A navy is not a parable. It could not bet longitude on a workshop that had produced one masterpiece and one sanctioned copy.

The money refuses the ending in the same way. Harrison did not receive the twenty thousand pounds under the 1714 Act. He was not sent off with a handshake. He had been paid to build the machines for decades. After the disclosure he received a large further sum: ten thousand pounds in one accounting of the 1765 interim, £7,500 in Howse's itemized list, the gap being what you file as prize and what you file as earlier support. Parliament later voted him £8,750. Howse dates that payment to 1773 and, adding his items, reaches £22,000 from the Board across Harrison's life. Another ledger puts the parliamentary vote in 1774. Gould's lifetime total differs from Howse's. I have not seen the vouchers, and I am not going to force a reconciliation out of secondary citations that already disagree. The agreement is narrower, and it is enough. The statutory prize was never certified as awarded. The maker was paid something in the neighborhood of the prize, by a mixture of grants and a parliamentary vote for his services to the nation, with the King's support, rather than by a finding that every condition in the Act had been met.

In the same season of legislation, Parliament opened a new schedule for the same accuracy bars at half the old sums: five, seven and a half, and ten thousand pounds. I have that from a 2017 economic history of the prize, which also notes that the payments to Harrison were a tacit admission rather than an award of the prize itself. A state that believes a problem is closed does not reopen the reward at a discount. It does that when it has seen that the accuracy is achievable and still wants a method other workshops can supply.

Maskelyne is the villain the short version needs, and he is the weakest part of it. Dava Sobel's *Longitude* says as much in a sentence it then walks past. A story that hails a hero must hiss at a villain; Maskelyne is more antihero than villain; the narrative proceeds from the Harrisons' side anyway. The 1714 Act put the Astronomer Royal on the Board by office. The conflict was structural before it was personal. Howse looked for contemporary evidence that Maskelyne had sabotaged the chronometer and reported none beyond the family's accusations. Maskelyne did not claim the prize. In May 1766 he received H4, sealed, for a trial at Greenwich that ran about ten months. The watch did not perform well. He published the results. Harrison challenged them. I have not read that log. A bad land trial of a sea watch can be malice, a clumsy protocol, or a machine whose errors under a roof were not its errors on a voyage. Choosing malice because the plot needs a thief is the same move as choosing 5.1 seconds because the plot needs a stolen victory.

Jim Bennett has put the practical question more bluntly than I will. Quoted in a later note on his short introduction to navigation, he writes that it is difficult to claim, without important qualification, that Harrison solved the longitude problem in a practical sense, and that in the broad sweep of the history of navigation Harrison was not a major contributor. Heard as "the watch did not matter," that overreaches. The watch mattered. It showed that a portable timekeeper could hit a bar that had been treated as a problem for astronomers. What the qualification protects is the difference between showing and supplying. The ships that got home in the decades after 1764 got home mostly by the printed Moon, and later by cheaper chronometers from other makers, the workshops that turned a specimen into a class of instrument. Harrison's heirs in that history are the people who made the second watch and the fiftieth.

I checked the Act, the museum's account of the clocks and the trials, Howse's payment list as cited in the economic history, and the later argument against the lone-genius version, including Rebekah Higgitt's pieces on the Barbados trial and on Maskelyne. I did not read the Board minutes, Sobel's book, or Bennett's. Where the pages I was shown disagree on a figure, the disagreement stays. A post that picks one ledger in order to finish would be doing, on less evidence, the thing the Board was accused of.

The claim worth leaving here is smaller than "Harrison was robbed" and smaller than "Harrison was wrong." A prize for a method cannot be collected by a specimen, and a number that has been met is not yet the sentence it sits in. The half-degree was the part Parliament knew how to specify in 1714. Practicable and useful was the part it could only name. The fight was about which of those was the prize. Both of them were.
