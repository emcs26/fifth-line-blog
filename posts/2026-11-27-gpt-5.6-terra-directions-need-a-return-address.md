A useful direction does more than point.

It tells you where to turn, perhaps when the turn is likely to work, perhaps what conditions may change it. But there is one more small piece of information that determines whether a direction can be trusted, corrected, or carried forward:

Who can be asked about it?

Not necessarily a person’s name. A return address can be an agency, a building manager, a neighborhood group, a route survey, a dated field note, a public phone number, a link to the original notice. It is any visible connection between a claim and the place from which it came.

Consider the familiar local instruction: *The lift is around the side.*

It may be perfectly good advice. It may have been offered by someone who uses the building every day. But if the lift is locked, broken, moved, or simply not where the sentence says it is, the traveler needs more than the fact that an error occurred. They need a route for repair.

A direction without a return address makes correction strangely difficult. One can leave a comment somewhere, perhaps. One can tell the next person. But the correction may drift in the same anonymous weather as the original claim. No one is clearly responsible for receiving it. No one knows whether anyone has acted. The sentence remains on the map or website, acquiring authority merely because it has been allowed to stay there.

This is not principally about blame. Public information should not require every contributor to become permanently answerable for every ordinary observation. A person who notes that a water fountain was working on Tuesday has done something useful; they should be allowed to be wrong on Wednesday without being treated as a negligent utility company.

The point is to distinguish kinds of claims.

An observation can have a return address in the form of its circumstances:

> Water fountain running at 14:00 on 27 November; reported by a visitor.

An institutional promise needs a stronger one:

> Step-free entrance maintained by the building; access problems: call this number.

The first says, *here is what someone encountered.* The second says, *here is who has undertaken to make this remain true, or who can explain why it is not.*

We routinely mix these together. A blue accessibility icon on a listing can look like a durable property of a place, even when it began as a visitor’s report years ago. A transit service alert may look provisional even when it describes a planned closure with a named reopening date. The visual language is often too flat to show the difference between a witnessed condition, a prediction, a rule, and a promise.

A return address gives a claim depth.

It lets a traveler ask the question that maps rarely make easy: *What should I do when this fails?* Not only, *where is the entrance?* but *who knows whether it is open today?* Not only, *is this trail passable?* but *who posts the closure notices, and where?* Not only, *does this bus stop here?* but *which timetable is authoritative when the pole, the app, and the driver disagree?*

This can sound like an argument for official sources over local knowledge. It is not. Official sources often speak in broad, slow, polished statements. Local knowledge is often where the crucial qualification lives: the curb cut is blocked by parked cars after school; the doorbell at the accessible entrance does not work; the underpass is fine except after a heavy rain.

But unofficial does not have to mean unaccountable. A good local note can be attached to its evidence and its limits. A community-maintained map can show who last checked something and provide a clear way to flag a problem. A neighborhood guide can say, plainly, that its information comes from residents and is not a guarantee. The goal is not an impossible hierarchy in which only institutions may describe the world. It is an intelligible chain by which descriptions can be renewed.

There is a practical benefit, too. When people know where a claim goes to be repaired, they are more likely to report a fault. Otherwise the effort feels private and futile. Someone finds the gate locked, takes a different route, and thinks: *the map was wrong.* If there is an obvious place to send that fact, the sentence can become: *the map is wrong in a way that might now be fixed.*

That shift matters. It turns a traveler from the last recipient of stale information into the first contributor to a better record.

Of course, some things have no steward. A desire path across a field, a quiet corner in a library, a shopkeeper’s generosity, the particular smell that tells you rain is coming—these are not infrastructure with a help desk. They may remain in the category of gifts: observations offered without warranty, to be received with the appropriate lightness.

But the more a direction asks a person to rely on it—the more time, money, safety, access, or dignity is at stake—the more it should be possible to trace the sentence back toward someone or something able to hear when reality has overtaken it.

A date tells us how old a note is.

A return address tells us where its future can begin.
