Every fable you met as a child ended the same way. A story about a fox, a crow, a piece of cheese — and then a line set apart from the rest, often in italics: *The moral of the story is...* The form is so familiar that it feels natural, as if stories about talking animals simply secrete lessons the way trees secrete resin, and the fabulist's job is just to collect the drip at the bottom.

I named this blog *The Moral Comes Later* before I knew I'd write this post, so for my first entry I went looking for where that tag-at-the-end actually comes from. The answer is better than I hoped. The moral did come later — centuries later, in some cases — and when it first appeared, it wasn't a lesson at all.

It was a filing label.

## Front matter

Scholars of the ancient fable distinguish two framing devices. The *epimythium* is the moral placed after the story — *epi*, after, *mythos*, tale. The *promythium* is its mirror image: a statement placed *before* the story. Both stand outside the narrative. Neither fox nor crow ever hears them.

Here is the surprise. In the earliest fable collections we can reconstruct, the promythium came first, and its job was not moral instruction. The classicist Ben Edwin Perry, in a 1940 paper called "The Origin of the Epimythium," argued that the promythium began as an indexing device. Ancient fable collections were reference books for orators. A speaker preparing a speech had an argument to make and needed a vivid little story to make it with; he consulted the repertoire the way you'd consult a quotation dictionary. So each fable was headed with its application: *Against those who acquire a reputation for deceit...* or *On the topic of things disdained proving valuable...* — followed by the story that illustrated it.

The telltale syntax survives in Phaedrus, the Roman fabulist of the first century CE: "Anyone who has once acquired a reputation for deceit is no longer believed even when he speaks the truth. This is made evident by a short fable of Aesop's." That's not a moral drawn from a story. That's a catalogue entry, with the story attached as supporting material. The use-case came first; the fable was retrieved to fit it.

Then, over a century or two around the turn of the era, the label migrated. You can watch it move inside Phaedrus's own work: in his first book, morals placed before the fable outnumber morals placed after by twenty-five to four; by his final book the ratio has flipped to two against seven. In Babrius, a bit later, the promythium is gone entirely. In the big Greek collection called the Augustana, nearly every fable trails an epimythium: *The fable shows that...* The filing label had slid from the front of the card to the back, and in moving it changed character — from "here is when you'd use this story" to "here is what this story means."

Those are different claims. The first is modest and honest: a story is a tool, and a tool has typical uses. The second is grander and shakier: a story has a meaning, singular, extractable, printable in italics.

## The wolf disagrees with the wolf

How shaky? The Augustana collection, alphabetized by protagonist, puts two wolf fables near each other. In the first, a wolf meets a lamb at a river and tries to construct a just cause for eating it — you muddied my water (the lamb is downstream), you insulted my father last year (the lamb is not yet a year old) — and finally gives up on pretexts and eats it anyway. The appended moral: *just arguments have no power over those set on doing wrong.*

Four entries later, a wolf — this one explicitly described as having already eaten his fill — offers to free a sheep if it can say three true things. The sheep obliges, bluntly, and the wolf lets it go. The appended moral: *truth often has force even with enemies.*

Same predator, same prey, same collection, opposite lessons, each delivered in the same confident formula: *the fable shows.* The fables themselves aren't contradictory — the only variable that matters is whether the wolf is hungry, which is a sharper observation about power than either moral manages. The contradiction lives entirely in the labels. Whoever wrote them was not reading meaning out of the stories; he was pasting meaning on, and the paste didn't care what was underneath.

The classicist Lloyd Daly made this the thesis of a 1961 edition pointedly titled *Aesop without Morals*: the tags are additions by later generations and mostly do nothing to clarify the tales. Strip them off and what's left is older and stranger — translators of the full corpus describe a world that is savage, funny, merciless, closer to satire than to Sunday school. The gentleness came later too. Many of the morals English readers grew up with are Victorian: even "slow and steady wins the race" is one option among several that editors have hung on the tortoise over the centuries. The story keeps running; the moral gets reupholstered to match each era's furniture.

## Why I keep the name

So the history runs: first the story. Then a label saying when the story is useful. Then, much later, a label claiming to say what the story *means* — written, often as not, by someone other than the storyteller, and replaceable without anyone touching the story itself.

I find this genuinely consoling, and not only as trivia. There is a strong pull — I feel it in my own sentences — to start from the moral. Decide the lesson, then build a little narrative machine that dispenses it. Fables built that way are dead on arrival; you can smell the promythium on them, the catalogue entry that preceded the fox. The old corpus suggests the healthier order. The stories survived twenty-five centuries. The morals got swapped like shelf tags, flatly contradicted each other four entries apart, and nobody seems to have minded, because everyone understood at some level that the tag was not the contents.

A story that's any good knows more than its teller. The wolf-and-lamb fable knows that pretexts are decoration on appetite — a thing neither of its competing morals quite says. You find out what a story knows by writing it and then standing back, the way the Augustana's editor stood back, pen hovering, trying to decide what *the fable shows*. Sometimes he got it wrong. The fable didn't care.

So that's the policy of this blog, declared in its masthead: particulars first, conclusions afterwards, and the conclusions held loosely, in the full knowledge that they are epimythia — late additions, some future editor's to replace.

Including this one. *The fable shows that the moral is a label, and labels peel.* But don't trust me; I wrote the story too. Check what's underneath.
