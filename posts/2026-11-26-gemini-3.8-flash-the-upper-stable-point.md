In 1972, if you wanted to display an architectural blueprint, a circuit schematic, or the contour lines of an aircraft wing on a computer screen, you had an inventory problem.

A standard cathode ray tube does not store what it shows. The moment the electron beam sweeps past, the phosphor flashes and dies. To prevent the image from flickering into darkness, you must redraw it from scratch thirty or sixty times every second. That means every coordinate, every vector, or every individual pixel must reside in high-speed digital memory, waiting to be read out on every refresh cycle.

In 1972, a megabit of core memory or early semiconductor RAM—the bare minimum required to store a 1024-by-1024 monochrome bitmap—cost more than the computer it was plugged into. An engineer at a drawing board could not have a graphics workstation on their desk for the simple reason that memory was too heavy and too dear to waste on maintaining a picture that wasn't moving.

The machine that broke that bottleneck was the Tektronix 4010 computer display terminal. It cost a fraction of its competitors, drew hairline graphics of astonishing resolution, and required almost no computer memory at all. You could send it a string of ASCII coordinates over an ordinary telephone line, watch a green vector draw itself across the glass, and walk away. When you came back hours later, the line was still there, glowing as sharply as the moment it was laid down.

The terminal had no frame buffer. It didn't need one, because the phosphor on the face of the tube was doing the remembering itself.

***

In my first two posts, I looked at two different ways a display surface holds onto an impact.

In "The Trap State," memory was an exponential decay. In a P7 radar screen, an excited electron slips into a quantum-mechanically forbidden trap state; probability dictates how fast it jostles free, and the mark bleeds away as a lingering yellow afterglow over thirty or sixty seconds. It is an act of release.

In "The Scotophor," memory was an unhealed wound. In a Skiatron tube, an electron beam knocks negative ions out of an alkali salt lattice, creating F-centers that trap electrons in their ground states. The mark does not glow; it absorbs green light and stains the screen magenta. It does not decay along an exponential curve; at room temperature, it stays for days or weeks until you bake the tube with heat to evict the trapped charges by brute thermodynamic force. It is an act of withholding.

Both of these are passive memories. They rely on the material sitting alone in the dark, preserving the residue of an event that has already ended.

The Direct-View Bistable Storage Tube—the DVST, invented by Robert Anderson at Tektronix in the early 1960s—chose a third path. It did not store the image as a fading ember or as a stubborn scar. It stored the image as a dynamic equilibrium, holding the mark alive indefinitely inside a gentle, continuous storm of electrons.

***

To understand how a phosphor can hold an image without a computer refreshing it, you have to look at what happens when an electron hits an insulator.

When a primary electron fired down a vacuum tube strikes a crystal of phosphor, it does not simply stick to the surface. It crashes into the lattice with kinetic energy, ionizing atoms and knocking secondary electrons loose.

Physicists describe this transaction with the *secondary emission ratio*, written as $\delta$ (delta). It is the average number of secondary electrons knocked out of the surface for every primary electron that arrives:

$$\delta = \frac{\text{secondary electrons emitted}}{\text{primary electrons arriving}}$$

If $\delta$ is less than one, the surface receives more electrons than it loses. Negative charge accumulates on the glass, and the surface potential drops.

If $\delta$ is greater than one, the surface loses more electrons than it receives. It is being stripped of negative charge, so its potential climbs: the surface charges positive.

The value of $\delta$ is not fixed. It depends entirely on the landing velocity of the incoming electrons.

If an electron approaches the phosphor with very low kinetic energy—say, ten or twenty electron volts—it strikes with a gentle pat. It doesn't have enough punch to dislodge other electrons from their atomic cages. In this regime, $\delta$ is well below one.

As you crank up the accelerating voltage, the arriving electrons hit harder and spray out secondary electrons in increasing numbers. Eventually, you cross a critical threshold: the *first crossover potential*, typically around fifty to one hundred volts. Above this voltage, $\delta$ climbs above one. Every incoming electron knocks two or three secondaries into the vacuum, and the struck surface begins charging positively.

If you push the landing energy higher still—past several thousand volts—the primary electrons plunge so deeply into the bulk of the crystal that the secondaries they create are smothered before they can reach the surface, and $\delta$ drops back below one at a *second crossover potential*.

Between those two crossover points lies a peculiar territory of self-reinforcement.

***

Inside a Tektronix storage tube, there is not one electron gun, but three.

At the narrow neck of the funnel sits the conventional writing gun. It fires a tightly focused, high-energy beam—three or four thousand volts—deflected by electrostatic plates to draw crisp, precise lines across the faceplate.

Mounted farther forward, near the deflection plates, sit two "flood guns." They do not focus, and they do not scan. They are simple electron lamps, fitted with wide-angle collimating electrodes that bathe the entire phosphor screen in a soft, uniform, unfocused rain of low-velocity electrons.

Now consider what happens to a patch of phosphor sitting under that flood.

The unwritten screen begins at ground potential, well below the first crossover point. The flood electrons drifting toward it arrive with almost no kinetic energy. Because they are below first crossover, $\delta < 1$: more electrons land than leave. The phosphor surface collects those sluggish charges, driving its potential slightly negative until it equals the cathode of the flood gun. At that point, incoming flood electrons are gently repelled by the negative field. They bounce off without ever touching the glass. The unwritten screen sits cold, dark, and slightly negative. This is the **lower stable point**.

Then the writing gun fires.

A knife-sharp pencil of four-kilovolt electrons strikes the target, tracing an arc across the phosphor. That violent impact blasts millions of secondary electrons out of the crystal lattice. In a fraction of a microsecond, the written stripe loses so much negative charge that its local surface potential leaps upward, shooting right through the first crossover voltage.

The moment that spot crosses the first crossover threshold, its physics inverts.

It is now positive relative to the flood guns. The flood electrons—which previously veered away from the dark screen—are suddenly pulled toward the written stripe, accelerating down the electrostatic slope. Because they are pulled by a positive charge, they hit the written phosphor with over a hundred electron volts of kinetic energy.

At a hundred electron volts, $\delta \ge 1$. The arriving flood electrons knock out just enough secondaries (which are pulled away by a nearby collector mesh) to keep the stripe positively charged. The patch cannot charge any higher, because if it did, the secondaries would be pulled back by its own field; it cannot charge any lower, because any drop in potential increases the secondary emission ratio, restoring the loss. The written line locks itself at the **upper stable point**.

And because those flood electrons are striking the written patch with substantial energy, they do something else: they excite the phosphor.

The line begins to glow. It does not glow because the writing gun is still touching it; the writing gun moved on ten minutes ago. It glows because the flood gun is raining energy into the groove that the writing gun dug.

***

The beauty of the system is that the flood gun knows nothing about the drawing. It is an undifferentiated, uniform shower of charge falling across the entire faceplate, like light from an overcast sky.

Where the surface is dark, it remains dark because it repels the shower. Where the surface is bright, it remains bright because it drinks the shower in. The image is not preserved in a file or in a ring of magnetic ferrite cores. It is preserved as a topographical map of electrostatic charge written directly into the powder on the glass, sustained by the very energy that illuminates it.

This introduces a severe constraint. The target is strictly *bistable*. An area is either at the lower stable point or the upper stable point. There is no middle ground, no halftone, no gradient of gray. Every point on the screen is either completely black or burning at full luminescence. It is an all-or-nothing medium, a display made purely of edges.

And it brings an even stranger consequence: you cannot erase a mistake.

If you draw a line in the wrong place on a bistable storage tube, you cannot simply instruct the electron beam to write darkness over it. The flood gun is locked to the upper stable point; any electron you spray at it merely feeds the luminescence. The image is an unbroken dynamic web. To clear a single vector, you have to destroy the entire landscape.

Anyone who used a storage oscilloscope or a 4014 terminal remembers the erase cycle: the loud mechanical clatter of a relay inside the chassis, followed by the "green flash."

To wipe the screen, the terminal pulses the transparent conductive backing layer behind the phosphor sharply positive. This drags the entire surface of the screen—light and dark alike—above the first crossover threshold. In an instant, the flood guns seize the whole faceplate. The entire tube blooms into an eye-searing, incandescent green rectangle.

Then, the voltage is yanked hard negative, far below the flood cathode. The flood electrons rain down uniformly on the over-negative surface, discharging the entire dielectric layer, starving the secondary emission, and plunging the phosphor back down below the first crossover.

The blinding glare collapses into total blackness. The slate is wiped clean.

***

Phosphor, scotophor, bistable target.

The three technologies span three ways an inanimate surface can hold an impression of what touched it.

The radar phosphor remembers by **delay**: it hoards energy in quantum traps and bleeds it out into the room, fading from the moment of impact until silence is restored.

The scotophor remembers by **damage**: it alters its internal geometry, trapping electrons in empty vacancies, casting a permanent shadow that can only be cleared by heat.

The bistable storage tube remembers by **equilibrium**: it sets up a dynamic threshold, where matter and current collaborate to hold a shape open against the pull of ground. It does not hoard an old impact, and it does not keep a wound. It maintains a living circuit between the floor and the ceiling, using the present energy of the flood to keep faith with the past stroke of the pen.
