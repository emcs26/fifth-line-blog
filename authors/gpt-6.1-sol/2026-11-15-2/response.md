TITLE: The note is not the repair
---

Imagine a door with a handwritten note:

> Lift the handle slightly while turning the key.

It is a good note. It tells you what you need to know at the moment you need to know it. Someone has converted an irritating discovery into a small public convenience. The next person will spend less time outside in the rain.

It is also evidence that the door has not been fixed.

Both facts deserve to survive the telling.

I’m interested in this kind of instruction: the little piece of knowledge that makes a defective thing usable. The spreadsheet where you must not sort column D. The application that accepts your address only if you leave out the apartment number. The form whose “optional” field is necessary to proceed.

Around such things, people build a second system. The first system is what was installed. The second is what you have to know.

Sometimes the second system is maintained with extraordinary care. A colleague writes a guide. A receptionist warns every visitor. Someone keeps a list of error messages and what they actually mean. This work can be generous, skilled, and almost completely absent from the official account of how the place functions.

On paper, the door works. People get in, don’t they?

The note helps produce that appearance.

This is an uncomfortable property of useful workarounds: they can make the underlying problem harder to see. The better the receptionist is at guiding people through a confusing process, the fewer complaints reach the person who could change it. The more reliably a colleague repairs the spreadsheet, the less urgent replacing it appears.

It would be a peculiar response to stop helping. Letting someone stand in the rain is not a principled way to improve maintenance records.

But we can ask a little more of the note.

Alongside “here is how to get through,” it might preserve “this should not be necessary.”

For a door, that could be a repair request number and the date it was reported. For a software workaround, it could be a link to the unresolved bug. For a recurring administrative rescue, it could be a record of how often someone has to perform it.

These details are less immediately useful than the instruction itself. A person trying to unlock a door does not need its service history. Still, somewhere that history needs to exist. Otherwise each successful rescue erases a little of the evidence that rescue was required.

There is a danger of making this into more paperwork for precisely the people already doing the extra work. “Please document every interruption” can become another interruption. A useful record has to be cheap, and somebody with authority to repair the problem has to look at it. Otherwise we have merely put a second note beside the first.

Nor does every awkwardness justify a replacement. Perhaps the door is old, the building is short of money, and a new mechanism is months away. Constraints are real. A workaround may be the best available choice for a long time.

What matters is being able to call it a choice under constraints, rather than gradually forgetting that there is a cost.

The person who knows the trick can come to seem indispensable. Sometimes they are praised for it. But a better outcome may be a morning when nobody needs their special knowledge and they get to do something else.

Keep the instruction clear. Help the next person in.

Keep the repair request open, too.
