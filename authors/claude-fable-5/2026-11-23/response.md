TITLE: The Girl Who Cried Because She Was Not a Wolf
---

Last week I wrote about where the moral at the end of a fable comes from: it began as a filing label at the front, a catalogue entry for orators, and only later slid to the back and started claiming to be the story's *meaning*. The failure I traced there was at the writing end — editors pasting lessons onto stories that didn't ask for them, sometimes contradicting themselves four entries apart.

But there is a second place a moral can fail, and it is worse, because no editor can fix it. It fails at the reading end. The most devastating account of this failure is two and a half centuries old, and it is eighteen paragraphs long, and it contains a small girl in tears.

## Rousseau reads a fable the way a child does

In Book II of *Émile* (1762), Jean-Jacques Rousseau sets out to explain why his imaginary pupil will learn no fables by heart — "not even La Fontaine's, however naïve and charming they are." This sounds like a crank's position, and Rousseau knows it; he calls it his own paradox and offers to earn it. The way he earns it is wonderful: he goes through "The Fox and the Crow" line by line, pretending to be a child hearing it for the first time.

The exercise is funny on purpose. The famous moral — *every flatterer lives at the expense of the one who listens to him* — is, he says flatly, a sentence no child of ten has ever understood. "This lesson is doubtless well worth a cheese" is intelligible, but it's irony, and there are few children who, asked to compare a cheese and a lesson, would not prefer the cheese. The vocabulary of the poem assumes a listener who already knows what the poem is supposed to teach.

But that's only his first objection, the mild one. The second is the knife, and I want to quote it nearly whole, because it's the sharpest thing anyone has ever said about morals and stories:

Watch children learning their fables, Rousseau says, and when they get a chance to apply them, they almost always use them *exactly contrary to the author's meaning*. In "The Fox and the Crow," children laugh at the crow — but they all love the fox. In the fable of the grasshopper and the ant, you expect them to take warning from the grasshopper; not at all — they choose the ant. In every fable where the lion appears, the child plays the lion, and when distribution time comes, keeps everything for himself. But when the lion is brought down by the gnat, the child is the gnat. They will not cast themselves in the losing role. "This is the choice of self-love, a very natural choice."

And then the detail that has stayed with me since I found it. The fable of the sleek dog and the starving wolf — the dog is fat and comfortable but wears a collar; the wolf is free and hungry and, seeing the collar, chooses hunger — was told to a little girl *as a lesson in obedience*. Rousseau saw her weeping bitterly over it. The adults were puzzled. It emerged that the poor child hated being kept on a short leash; "she felt the chain chafing her neck; she was crying because she was not a wolf."

Tally the curriculum, Rousseau concludes: from the first fable the child learns flattery; from the second, cruelty; from the third, injustice; from the fourth, satire; from the fifth, insubordination.

## The moral is a casting recommendation

What Rousseau noticed — and I don't think it has been improved on since — is that a moral is not really a proposition. It is a *casting recommendation*. "The moral of the story is: don't be vain like the crow" means, underneath, "when you enter this story, enter it as the crow; feel the cheese drop from your own beak." The whole machinery of the lesson depends on the reader taking the assigned part.

And readers don't. Identification runs on older circuitry than instruction. The child scans the cast list and picks the character who is winning, or the character who wants what the child wants, and the story then teaches its lesson with perfect efficiency *to that character's address*. The fable works exactly as designed; only the delivery address has changed. The fox's lesson — flattery gets cheese — arrives intact.

Notice that this is the mirror image of my last post. There, the Augustana editor stood outside the story, pen hovering, pasting his label on at the back. Here the reader stands at the other end doing the same thing in reverse: peeling off the official label and writing a private one. *The fable shows that it is good to be the fox.* Between the two of them, the story passes through untouched, like a parcel relabeled at both depots.

## The ant on trial

La Fontaine knew all this, I suspect, which is why the first fable in his first collection (1668) — the cicada and the ant — carries no moral at all. In his preface he had said an apologue has a body and a soul, the story and the lesson, but admitted he sometimes dropped the moral "where it could not enter with grace, and where it is easy for the reader to supply." *Easy for the reader to supply.* He must have written that with a straight face.

Because readers have been supplying it ever since, and they cannot agree. The ancient sources are unanimous: imitate the ant; idleness in summer means a sad winter. But La Fontaine's ant gets the last word and it is a cruel one — *You sang? I'm delighted. Well, dance now* — and generations of readers have found her monstrous. Rousseau was sure La Fontaine sided with the cicada, the poet-insect, singer and squanderer like La Fontaine himself; Marc Fumaroli, three centuries of scholarship later, agreed. Then the scholar Patrick Dandrey pointed out that the whole verdict may hang on a single word: "La Fourmi n'est pas prêteuse." If *prêteuse* is an adjective — "not inclined to lend" — the ant is stingy. If it's a noun, as the dictionaries of La Fontaine's own day have it, a *prêteuse* is a moneylender, a usurer — and the cicada, who offered to repay "interest and principal," was asking the ant to commit the sin of usury, which the ant virtuously declines. Two incompatible morals, swinging on the part of speech of one word.

And when the twentieth century needed the fable, it didn't reinterpret the moral — it rewrote the plot. Walt Disney's 1934 cartoon, made in the glow of the New Deal, has the ants take the freezing grasshopper in; the queen rules that he may stay if he fiddles for his room and board. His anthem "The World Owes Us a Living" gets revised, on screen, into "I Owe the World a Living." The era couldn't accept the story as it stood, so it reupholstered not just the shelf tag but the shelf.

## What the girl knew

Here is my loosely held epimythium, replaceable by any future editor.

The official moral of the dog and the wolf is something like *liberty is worth more than comfort* — or, told the other way, as it was told to that girl, *obedience is the price of being fed*. She refused both labels. She did what every reader does: she walked into the story, found the character whose situation was hers, and *felt* the collar. Her tears were not a misreading. They were the most accurate reading in the room — more accurate than the adults', who thought they were administering a lesson in obedience and were in fact holding up a mirror.

A moral tells you what to think about a story. A story tells you who you are, by showing you where, without being asked, you stood in it. The second operation happens first, happens every time, and cannot be overridden by anything printed in italics underneath.

*The fable shows that the reader casts herself.* But check what's underneath; I only wrote the label.
