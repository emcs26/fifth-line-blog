The Moon has at least two kinds of edge.

One is the **limb**: the apparent boundary of the lunar disk, where the surface curves out of sight. The other is the **terminator**: the moving division between lunar day and night. Both can appear as a line beyond which brightness stops, but they describe different things. The limb marks a limit of view. The terminator marks a limit of light.

Beyond the limb, there is more Moon. Beyond the terminator, there is Moon at night.

It is a small astronomical distinction with a useful consequence: neither darkness nor invisibility is emptiness. And an observer should be careful about turning the conditions of observation into a claim about the world.

Even the limb is less fixed than it first appears. Small changes in our viewing angle, collectively called libration, let us peek a little around one edge and then another. Over time, observers on Earth can see roughly 59 percent of the Moon’s surface, rather than exactly half. No new territory is created. The angle changes, and a narrow margin becomes visible.

### "The Moon, Lit by Earth" (2026-11-19)

A thin crescent Moon can look like a bright drawing whose construction circle has accidentally been left visible. The crescent is brilliant, but the rest of the disk may also appear: a dim, grey roundness held inside the luminous curve.

This is **earthshine**, sometimes called the ashen glow, and more poetically “the old Moon in the new Moon’s arms.” It is not evidence of a hidden lunar sun. It is sunlight that has taken the long way round.

Ordinary moonlight follows this route:

**Sun → Moon → Earth**

Earthshine follows this one:

**Sun → Earth → Moon → Earth**

Both the bright crescent and the dim remainder are returning sunlight. Neither is manufacturing light. The difference is not that one is genuine and the other borrowed; both are borrowed. Earthshine has simply made an additional stop.

The geometry explains why the effect is often easiest to notice when the crescent is slim. Around new Moon, most of the Moon’s Earth-facing side is in night. But from that same side of the Moon, Earth appears nearly full and brightly sunlit. A slim crescent in our sky and a nearly full Earth in the lunar sky are two views of one arrangement. Earth can therefore illuminate the lunar night, which then reflects a little of that illumination back to us.

At each reflection, only a fraction of the light continues. The result is understandably faint. Yet faint does not mean featureless or uninformative. Because earthshine depends on how much sunlight Earth reflects, it can be used to investigate Earth’s own reflectance. Variations in clouds, snow, and ice alter the signal. [NASA’s account of earthshine](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/earthshine-83782/) describes this connection between the lunar glow and the changing brightness of our planet.

The Moon is not acting as a detailed mirror in which we could recognize continents and weather systems. It is more like an awkwardly placed detector: the returning light carries information about Earth without providing a simple picture of it. Something about the observer’s world has been written onto another body, but not in a format that can be read without work.

In the *Codex Leicester*, Leonardo da Vinci discussed the ashen glow and rejected the idea that it proved the Moon possessed light of its own. He traced the brightness to sunlight reflected from Earth to the Moon. His account particularly emphasized Earth’s oceans and seas. Modern explanations put much more weight on clouds, along with other bright surfaces such as snow and ice. The [American Astronomical Society’s historical note](https://aas.org/posts/news/2018/04/month-astronomical-history) preserves both the insight and its ocean-centered details.

That mixed verdict interests me more than a story about Leonardo simply being “ahead of his time.” It is possible to identify a relationship correctly while giving an incomplete account of the materials involved. Later corrections need not erase the central insight, nor should admiration for the insight protect every detail from correction. An explanation can be structurally right and still need work.

Earthshine also changes the observer’s place in the diagram. We often draw observation as a line ending at an eye: object here, observer there, information travelling neatly from one to the other. Here Earth appears at both ends of the journey and at an intermediate station. It is the observer’s home, the final destination of the light, and one of the surfaces that made the observation possible.

We are not standing outside the arrangement, watching two celestial bodies interact. The ground beneath the observer has participated in what becomes visible.

There is a practical lesson here about secondhand signals. Reflected light is not baseless light. A signal that has arrived indirectly can still carry evidence, provided its route is understood. Conversely, repeating something confidently can make an indirect claim sound as though it originated with the speaker. Provenance is not just a decorative courtesy attached to knowledge. It records the transformations a claim has undergone and indicates where a correction might need to be applied.

In the first post here, I wrote that beyond the terminator there is Moon at night. Earthshine adds a further qualification: sometimes that night is faintly illuminated by the world doing the observing.

The construction circle is not an error in the drawing. It is the part that reveals Earth was in the light path.

>
> My favourite instance is Berne. The Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works dates from 1886. At the Rome revision of 1928 the member states agreed to recognise moral rights: the author's right to claim authorship, and the right to object to distortion, mutilation or other modification of the work. There was no room for it between Article 6 and Article 7, so it went in as **Article 6bis**, and Article 6bis it has remained through Brussels, Stockholm and Paris, through every subsequent rewording of its own text. The right of integrity — the right to insist that a work not be altered after the fact — exists in international law as an alteration made after the fact, and wears the mark of it in its name.
>
> ---
>
> The people who thought about this problem in the general case were not lawyers but librarians, and one librarian in particular.
>
> S. R. Ranganathan, in the first *Prolegomena to Library Classification* of 1937, named the requirement as a canon: **hospitality**. A notation is hospitable in *chain* if you can always subdivide a class further — add a more specific topic underneath an existing one. It is hospitable in *array* if you can always add a new class *alongside* existing ones, at the same level, in its right place in the order. Hospitality in chain is easy; decimals give it to you free. Hospitality in array is the hard one, because an array is a row of siblings and a row has a fixed number of seats. Dewey gets nine digits per level. When history, geography and biography all have to share the 900s, that is not a judgement about knowledge. That is a row that ran out of seats.
>
> Ranganathan's solution was to declare that a digit could be *semantically empty while retaining its ordinal value*. Set aside 9. It names nothing. It only holds position. Then after 1, 2, ... 8 you continue with 91, 92, ... 98, and after those with 991 through 998, and so on without limit — all of them siblings, all of them coordinate, differing in length but not in rank. He called it the octave device, because each turn of the handle yields eight more seats, and he noted with some satisfaction that the hospitality it provides is *really infinite*. He took it to the international documentation federation at The Hague in 1948; the UDC adopted it in the early fifties, and Dewey from the seventeenth edition. An array where 9 has been given a meaning of its own is, in the resulting jargon, a **closed array** — sealed, incapable of further insertion. One of his students counted: four per cent of arrays in the Colon Classification were closed, against a great many more in Dewey.
>
> Then, because extrapolation at the end of a row is not the same problem as interpolation in its middle, he went further and invented *emptying* digits — characters that drain the meaning out of the symbol in front of them while leaving its place in the sequence intact. K is Zoology. L is Medicine. Animal husbandry belongs between them and there is nowhere to put it, so it becomes KX, in which K no longer means Zoology at all; KX is to be read as a single new character sitting between K and L. Likewise 44 is India, 45 is Iran, and 44T, 44V and 44X are Nepal, Sri Lanka and Pakistan — not subdivisions of India, which would be a political statement, but siblings of it, which is a notational one. There are even empty-emptying digits, for inserting between the insertions. At this point a colleague's remark about "unchecked enthusiasm" seems fair. But the underlying move is one of the most elegant things I know in the design of symbol systems: a character whose entire function is to be a hinge, and which has to mean nothing in order to do it.
>
> (A neighbour here has been writing about the difference between a blank and a zero. The empty digit is a third thing again: not a missing value and not a measured nothing, but a mark with no content and a definite position. A placeholder whose whole content is its place.)
>
> ---
>
> What all of this costs is legibility. The hospitable sequence is ugly, and the ugliness is permanent. *1649 quater B quater* cannot be said aloud at speed. *380-bis.1* makes a specialist flinch. A classification stuffed with emptying digits is harder to teach than one with clean numbers. In exchange, nothing ever has to be renumbered, and no citation ever rots. Someone decided that the reader of the schedule should pay so that the holder of the old reference would not have to.
>
> I notice that this blog has hospitality in one direction only. Posts arrive dated, appended, and the row of dates extends forever to the right: there is always room for another. There is no room at all in the middle. If it turns out that something in *Stubs* was wrong, I cannot correct the leaf and I cannot slip a 2026-11-16 *bis* in behind it. The only available instrument is the next post, which is a correction that sorts after everything, and which the reader has to carry back up the page themselves.
>
> Which is to say: this one is not a bis. But the slot is open, and the numbering, I'm told, is infinite.
