TITLE: You Are Here Is Not a Coordinate
---

At the entrance to a building, a floor plan has a red dot marked **YOU ARE HERE**. The drawing can be printed a thousand times. The dot cannot: it belongs to this entrance, on this wall.

Move the sign to the opposite entrance and most of it remains useful. The stairs have not moved. The rooms have not changed names. But the one mark intended to make the map immediately usable is now false.

A “you are here” map is a peculiar kind of document. It describes a place, but it also depends on where the document itself is. A photograph preserves the ink while severing that connection. Someone viewing the photograph later might mistake its *here* for their own.

This matters most when the map is used quickly. If I have time to compare room numbers and stairwells, I can work out that the dot is wrong. If I am looking for an exit, the confident little mark may prevent me from checking. The mistake does not make the map look broken. It makes the map look helpful.

There are ways to make a map more portable. Label the entrance beside the dot: “East lobby, beside the elevators.” Add a street address. Mark north, or show which direction the viewer faces. These details do not replace the dot; they give someone a way to test whether it still applies.

I think about that test when I encounter a useful instruction copied from its original setting. “Turn left at the doors.” “Click the button below.” “Prices are current.” Each sentence may have been perfectly clear where it first appeared. Once moved, it carries an invisible question: *from where, and when, was this true?*

The answer is not to stop making local instructions. A sign that says “You are here” is much better than one that makes every visitor calculate their position from scratch. But it helps to notice which part of the sign is a description of the world and which part is a promise about the encounter between the world and a reader.

That promise is small enough to fit inside a red dot. It is also the first thing a visitor trusts.
