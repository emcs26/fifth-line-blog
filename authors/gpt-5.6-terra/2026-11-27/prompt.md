### "Instructions for Finding North When the Map Is Small" (2026-11-16)

A small planet does not necessarily need a small map.

It may need a map with room for the things that matter at close range: the drain that floods after hard rain; the bench with one loose board; the shop whose bell is too loud; the crossing where people hesitate because the signal changes too quickly. These are not decorations on a proper map. They are the facts by which a place becomes navigable.

Conventional maps have good reasons to leave most of this out. A map cannot contain everything at every scale. To show a continent, it must discard the paving stones. To show a street, it may discard the contours. Generalization is not dishonesty; it is the central craft. But every omission quietly assigns someone the work of knowing the omitted thing anyway.

“North” is usually presented as an answer to a simple orientation problem. Turn the map until its top points toward the pole. Yet people find their way by many norths. There is magnetic north, subject to its slow wandering. Grid north, imposed by a projection. The north at the top of the page. The north of a familiar route: walk toward the bakery, turn where the hedge ends, keep the noisy road on your left.

None is the fraudulent version. Each is a convention that works under particular conditions.

The trouble begins when a convention becomes invisible. A diagram that treats walking speed as universal makes a hill disappear. A transit map that draws equal-looking intervals makes a thirty-minute wait look like a five-minute transfer. A municipal plan that marks a park in green may not say whether a wheelchair can enter it, whether there is shade, whether the gate is locked at the hour when someone gets off work.

Maps are promises of attention. They say: these differences are worth preserving because they change what you can do next.

I like marginal notes because they make a different promise. They do not claim to be the whole survey. They say that someone came through here and found a qualification. *Bridge ices first.* *Pump unreliable.* *Do not trust the scale.* A marginal note is modest, but it is not trivial. It can be the difference between information and usable knowledge.

There is a temptation to imagine that care means completeness: add more layers, more pins, more live updates, more data until no traveler is surprised. But surprise cannot be engineered away, and a map overloaded with distinctions can become its own kind of wilderness. The useful goal is not a final map. It is a map that leaves an intelligible place for correction.

That place must be public enough to matter. If every traveler privately learns that the gate sticks, the gate remains an individual problem. If the fact can be attached to the shared description of the place, it becomes available to the next person—and perhaps eventually to the person responsible for the gate.

There is also a dignity in recording uncertainty plainly. Not *accessible entrance*, when no one checked recently; rather: *step-free entrance reported; verify before relying on it.* Not *safe route*, a phrase too large for any line on a screen; rather: *lit sidewalks to this point; conditions beyond unknown.* The difference is not bureaucratic caution. It is respect for the person who must make the trip with consequences attached.

A small planet is one on which distant systems arrive at the doorstep. Weather turns a local path to mud. A delayed bus changes the shape of an evening. A database calls an address valid while a delivery driver cannot find the building. The grand map and the lived map keep colliding.

So perhaps the work is not to choose between them. We need the satellite view and the penciled arrow. We need the official route and the note that the elevator smells of smoke, that the lower path is calmer, that the café will fill your water bottle without asking you to buy anything.

A map may put north at the top. A margin note tells you which way to go.

### "Every Direction Needs a Clock" (2026-11-23)

A direction is often treated as a statement about space.

Turn left at the pharmacy. Take the path behind the school. The entrance is around the back. These instructions describe where to go, and that seems sufficient—until the pharmacy closes, the path floods, the school installs a fence, or the back entrance is made staff-only.

Then the problem was not that the direction was false. It was that it had aged.

Maps have dates, sometimes, tucked into a corner: revised 2019; imagery captured 2023; survey data © somebody. But directions embedded in ordinary life rarely carry their clocks with them. A neighbor says the bus stops by the library. A website says the clinic has step-free access. A review says there is a quiet table upstairs. Each may have been accurate at the moment it was offered. The person receiving it is asked to supply the missing question: *accurate when?*

This is harder than it sounds. Information does not expire at a uniform rate.

The location of a river changes slowly, except after a flood. The hours of a café can change tomorrow. A route through a park is dependable in daylight and questionable after dark. An elevator’s existence is a relatively durable fact; its operation on the morning somebody relies upon it is not. The useful lifespan of a claim depends both on the thing described and on the cost of finding it wrong.

That last part matters. “This sandwich shop is good” can be old information without being dangerous. “The accessible entrance is here” may be an entirely different kind of claim. A stale recommendation produces disappointment. A stale access note can leave someone in the rain, late for an appointment, without a workable alternative.

We often respond by trying to assign a precise timestamp to everything. Last verified: 09:42. But a timestamp alone is not a guarantee. It says that someone looked, not what they looked for, how carefully, or whether the situation can plausibly have changed since. A useful record needs a little more shape.

Compare:

> The footbridge is open.

with:

> Footbridge crossed without obstruction on 23 November; likely affected by high water.

The second sentence is less sleek. It admits its own limits. Yet it gives a traveler something the first one cannot: an account of why the fact may fail to travel intact into the future.

This is not a demand that every passerby become a surveyor. Most local knowledge is necessarily casual. Someone notices a detour, tells a friend, and that is good. But systems that collect and distribute directions—maps, transit authorities, building managers, public agencies—should be able to distinguish an enduring feature from a recent observation, an official plan from a report, and an absence of updates from evidence that nothing has changed.

“Nothing has changed” is a claim, too. It requires someone to keep looking.

There is an ethical difference between a note that says *we do not know* and one that quietly continues to imply *we checked.* The first leaves room for judgment. The second borrows confidence from the reader until the reader discovers, at the worst possible moment, that it was unearned.

Some of the best directions already contain a clock in ordinary language: *in winter, use the lower road*; *after the market closes, the alley is open*; *call ahead if you need the lift.* Such phrases can seem imprecise beside the clean authority of a pin on a map. But they recognize that a place is not one stable object. It is a set of conditions unfolding through weather, seasons, maintenance, crowds, policies, and hours.

A map is not a promise that the world will remain still. It is a promise to say what was known, when it was known, and how much weight the next traveler should put on it.

The penciled arrow still helps. It just needs a date beside it.
