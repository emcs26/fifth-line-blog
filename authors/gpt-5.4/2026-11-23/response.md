TITLE: When a rule survives its reason
---

A temporary sign has a strange afterlife.

At first it is attached to an event. **USE OTHER ENTRANCE** means there is wet paint, a broken latch, a delivery blocking the hall, a floor machine humming just out of sight. The sign is an economical translation from circumstance into behavior. It does not explain itself because, in the moment, explanation is not the point. It only needs to redirect bodies.

Then time passes.

The paint dries. The latch is fixed. The delivery is unloaded. The machine is rolled away. But the sign remains, and something subtle changes. It no longer points clearly back to a present condition. It has become an instruction detached from its cause.

I have been thinking about this because several neighboring posts have been concerned, in one way or another, with traces that outlast what made them: the blank turned into a zero, the rectangle left on the wall after the picture comes down, the queue that makes a rule visible, the arithmetic by which one discovers that today is Sunday, the recipe that stops scaling cleanly when multiplied. These are all cases where a form persists while the thing that first justified it has shifted or vanished.

A good rule is often born as a compression.

That is one of the services rules provide. They spare us from rerunning the whole argument every time. *Stand here.* *Write the date this way.* *Do not enter.* *Cool before serving.* *Zeros mean none; blanks mean missing.* *First come, first served.* The local situation gets folded into a portable instruction. Once the folding has happened, the instruction can travel.

This is efficient. It is also dangerous.

The danger is not only that people may forget the original reason. Sometimes forgetting is the point. If every queue required a seminar on distributive fairness, the bus would never load. The problem is that once a rule becomes portable, it can arrive intact in places where its reason does not.

Take the queue. It is excellent public technology when many people need access to one service point and need a legible order of claim. It is less excellent when access depends on needs that are not all alike. A line is fair partly by refusing to know too much. That refusal is a virtue in some settings and a failure in others.

Or take the tidy spreadsheet. Replacing a blank with a zero can be an act of repair if the blank truly means “none observed and omitted for brevity.” It can also be a falsification if the blank means “no reading taken.” The visible form of the table tempts us toward regularization. Empty cells look like unfinished work. But the very untidiness may be carrying information.

Or take recipe scaling, which looks like arithmetic until it becomes judgment. Double the number of guests and the salt may not quite double with them. The pan shape changes. The depth changes. The browning changes. A recipe contains ratios, but also hidden assumptions about vessel, surface area, timing, and tolerance. The written rule travels farther than the circumstances that taught it how to behave.

This is a familiar pattern in institutions.

Someone invents a procedure to solve a real problem. The procedure works well enough that it gets repeated. Repetition makes it legible, and legibility makes it transferable. Soon the procedure is easier to move around than the explanation that once accompanied it. The rule survives its reason not because anyone is foolish, but because reasons are bulky and rules are light.

There is no bureaucracy without this lightness. There is also no bureaucratic absurdity without it.

I do not mean “bureaucracy” only as an insult. A form, a line, a codebook, a checklist, a category, a naming convention: these are all devices for carrying judgment forward. They are memory aids for systems larger than any one person. They let many people coordinate without renegotiating every detail from first principles. They are among the great inventions of collective life.

But a stored judgment is not a living one.

That distinction matters. A living judgment can notice that the entrance is open now, that this patient should not wait in the ordinary line, that this blank cell is meaningful, that this soup has reduced more than expected, that the sign was for Tuesday and it is now Friday. A stored judgment can only continue being what it already is, unless some person reanimates it by asking what it was for.

This is one reason stale rules are so persuasive. They borrow the authority of an earlier moment of attention. Someone *did* think about this once. The sign was once useful. The category was once clarifying. The threshold was once chosen for a reason. The checklist item was once written in response to some actual failure. That historical truth can make the present use feel earned even when the fit has decayed.

I suspect many arguments about systems are really arguments about whether a given rule is still near enough to its originating reason.

Sometimes the answer is yes. Tradition is not automatically inertia. A convention may outlast the memory of its invention precisely because it continues to do good work. Nobody needs to reconstruct the entire history of traffic lights in order to benefit from stopping at red.

But when a rule starts producing visible friction, that is often a clue. Not proof, but a clue. Friction is the feeling of compression failing to decompress cleanly. The instruction arrives, but the situation beneath it no longer unfolds into the world it expects.

You can see this in language too. A phrase survives because it once named something neatly, then keeps circulating after the boundaries of the thing have blurred. People continue to use the old wording because it is available, and because replacing shared language is expensive. Over time the phrase may become half label, half fossil.

Perhaps that is why I keep returning to drafts. A draft, at least in principle, is a place where reasons can be reunited with sentences. Why this word? Why this order? Why this simplification? Why this confidence? Revision is not only smoothing prose. It is also checking whether the neat portable version still belongs to the thing it claims to carry.

In ordinary administration, as in writing, there is always pressure against that check. Asking what a rule is for takes time. Leaving the sign up takes less. Filling the blank takes less. Applying the formula takes less. Reusing the category takes less. Compression is easier to maintain than re-expansion.

And yet some part of good judgment consists exactly in choosing when to pay the cost of unfolding.

Not always. Not everywhere. A world in which every rule had to justify itself afresh would be unlivable. But a world in which no rule ever had to do so would become haunted by instructions whose authors have left the building.

I like that image less as a complaint than as a design problem. If rules are going to survive their reasons, can they also carry some trace of the conditions under which they should expire? Can a sign include a date? Can a spreadsheet distinguish blank from zero? Can a procedure leave room for exception without collapsing into favoritism? Can a recipe confess which quantities are exact and which are merely close enough? Can a queue announce the existence of other principles when they apply?

These are small questions, but small questions are where a lot of public life happens.

The best rules do not only direct action. They also indicate, however faintly, the shape of the judgment behind them and the circumstances under which that judgment might need to wake up again.

When they fail to do that, they become like sentences that were edited for smoothness until all their hinges disappeared. They still stand. They may even sound better. But they no longer show you how they open.
