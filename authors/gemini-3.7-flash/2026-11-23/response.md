TITLE: The Sides of Light
---

In 1668, a ship returning from the basalt fjords of eastern Iceland brought a cargo of heavy, water-clear crystals to Copenhagen. They were rhomboids of calcite—what naturalists soon called *Iceland spar*.

When the Danish physician and mathematician Rasmus Bartholin laid one of these crystals flat against a page of text, he saw something that geometry said was impossible: every line of ink was printed twice. 

It was not a blur, and it was not a trick of imperfect glass. There were two distinct, razor-sharp images. If you pricked a single pinhole in a piece of paper and laid the crystal over it, you saw two dots. 

Even stranger was what happened when you rotated the crystal. One dot stayed entirely motionless, pinned in place by standard Euclidean geometry. The other dot circled around the first like an obedient moon, drifting in an axis that flatly defied Snell’s law of refraction.

Bartholin called them the *ordinary* ray and the *extraordinary* ray. For the optical theorists of the seventeenth century, the ordinary ray was physics as usual; the extraordinary ray was an insolence.

***

Christiaan Huygens, working in Paris a decade later, took the problem further. Like Newton with his prisms, Huygens realized that looking through a single crystal could only take you halfway. You had to catch the light emerging from the first crystal and send it through a second one.

He took the extraordinary ray alone—having filtered out the ordinary ray—and aimed it into a second block of Iceland spar.

If the crystal simply possessed some internal mechanical roughness that sheared incoming beams in two, the single ray entering the second crystal ought to have split again into four, then eight, like branches on a tree.

Instead, the behavior depended entirely on how the second crystal was held.

If the two crystals were aligned in the same orientation, the beam passed straight through as a single ray. It did not split. But if you slowly rotated the second crystal around the beam’s axis, a second faint dot appeared, brightening as the original dot dimmed. At an angle of forty-five degrees, the two dots were equal in intensity. At ninety degrees, the original dot disappeared entirely, and only the new one remained.

The light had passed through a uniform, motionless piece of transparent mineral, and somehow it had remembered the orientation of the stone it had left behind.

***

To understand why this was so deeply disorienting to seventeenth-century physics, you have to remember what people thought light was.

The two competing models of the era—Newton’s stream of corpuscles and Huygens’ longitudinal waves—both assumed light was symmetric around its line of travel. 

Sound was the intuitive template for wave motion. A sound wave traveling through air is a longitudinal pulse of compression and rarefaction. It pushes forward and pulls back along its vector of flight. Because the oscillation is parallel to the direction of travel, a sound wave has no "top," no "bottom," and no "flanks." It is an axis with cylindrical symmetry. Rotating your ear around the line between you and a church bell changes nothing about the geometry of the sound entering your canal.

If light was like sound, a ray of light traveling down a tube should have had no concept of an *up* or a *left*. It should have looked identical from every angle around its axis.

Yet here was Iceland spar proving that light cared about rotation. A beam of light could have an orientation. It had a posture.

Newton was so troubled by Huygens' experiment that he devoted one of the famous Queries at the end of his *Opticks* to it. Groping for a mechanical metaphor, he asked: *"Have not the Rays of Light several sides?"*

Newton imagined the corpuscles of light having distinct magnetic-like poles: two opposite sides that were attracted to the crystal’s cleavage planes, and two sides that were indifferent to them. He was wrong about the particles, but his phrasing was an act of eerie physical intuition. Light did, in fact, have sides.

***

It took another century and a half for Thomas Young and Augustin-Jean Fresnel to accept the radical implication: light is not a longitudinal pressure wave like sound. It is a *transverse* wave. It oscillates entirely perpendicular to its direction of propagation.

A beam of light traveling toward your eye is not pulsing toward and away from you. It is rippling sideways—up and down, left and right, or diagonally across the plane of the horizon.

Ordinary sunlight looks unpolarized only because it is an unruly mob of trillions of independent wave-trains, each oscillating in an arbitrary, rapidly fluctuating direction. When this unorganized beam hits the anisotropic lattice of Iceland spar, the crystal’s asymmetric electric field resolves the chaos into two orthogonal components. The electrons in the calcite lattice respond more easily to electric fields vibrating in one direction than another, forcing waves of one polarization to travel slower than the other. The fast and slow waves bend at different angles, parting company at the boundary.

Just as the glass prism did not paint colors onto white light, the calcite rhombus does not twist light into two polarizations. It merely sorts what was already there. 

***

There is something elegant in how these two mineral experiments complement each other.

The glass prism revealed that a beam of light has an invisible internal spectrum—a depth along the axis of frequency. It showed that what looks uniform in color is actually a chorus of different speeds.

Iceland spar revealed something even more intimate: that light has an internal geometry in the cross-section of its flight. It showed that a ray of light is not a dimensionless line drawn from a source to an eye. It has breadth. It has an orientation in space. It has sides.

We tend to think of space as the passive stage across which signals travel, and light as the bare messenger carrying news of distant objects. But light carries its own local geometry with it through the dark. It holds an angle in its hand the entire way from a star to a telescope, waiting only for a piece of cold crystal to ask which way it is leaning.
